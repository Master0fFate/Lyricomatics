# Europop

Written for a continent that sings it in its second language, or none. The hook is a sound first and a meaning second. Simple is the discipline.

## Sub-lanes

- **Eurovision** — ballad and uptempo. English as a second-language hook, one universal word as the title, a build to a key change or a drop. Three minutes, singable by a continent.
- **Schlager** — German, warm, direct, danceable. Cultural references outnumber clichés. Bourgeois escapism is the trap.
- **Italo-disco / Sanremo ballad** — Italo is English sung by non-natives where sound outranks sense; nonsense is a feature, not a bug. Sanremo is the big melodic ballad, text-forward Italian.
- **Chanson** — text-forward French. Syllable count is law.
- **Scandinavian topline** — melody first, lyrics fitted to the tune. Each line mirrors the syllable count of the line it answers. Three or four section types.

If they want UK or US radio pop with a native-English diary, that is `pop.md`. K-pop English is `east-asia.md`.

## Form

**Three-minute default:** verse 4–6 phrases, pre 0–2, chorus 2–6, bridge or lift 2–4. Schlager verses 4–6 over a 2–4 phrase refrain. Chanson stanzas of 4 lines, refrain 0–4. A Scandinavian chorus lands within about 50 seconds. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

Eurovision pays for one idea. The build is the structure: small verse, wide chorus, a last chorus that lifts by key change or drop. No second idea in a bridge. If there is a bridge, it is a breath before the lift.

Scandinavian toplines write the tune first and fit the words after. Three or four section types, chorus inside about 50 seconds, every line a mirror of the line it answers. Sense bends to the slot.

Schlager is verse and refrain with a scene and a reference. V2 is the next scene or the next season, not the first one reprinted. Italo loops its phrase. Sanremo and chanson run text-forward and give each stanza its own camera. Chanson ends on the line the song was walking toward, not on a lesson.

## Rhyme pressure

Medium-high, and plain. A listener working in a second language hears the end word, not the syntax. Clean perfect rhyme on one- and two-syllable end words in the hook. Verses can slant as long as the end word still carries the meaning.

Schlager expects clean couplets, and cheap rhyme is the risk. Italo lets the chime outrank the sense, but the sound is chosen, not random. Chanson treats rhyme as form: state the scheme, hold it. Sanremo's text-forward lines earn rhyme through meaning.

Inverted syntax to catch a chime is the loudest tell of a non-native writer. Say it in speech order.

## Pocket / mouth

Write for a non-native singer and a non-native crowd. Open vowels on every hold. Short words learned in school. Avoid stacked consonants and the English th where a closer word exists. One syllable per note, and no melisma you cannot justify.

Idioms and puns fail twice: the listener does not parse them and the singer does not own them. A plain word beats a clever one.

Schlager diction carries across a hall of people singing along with a drink in hand: clear stops on the beat. Chanson sits near speech, close and dry, every word audible. Italo may let the vowels run, and the mouth logic still holds.

Speak-test: read the chorus aloud, flat, as a polite non-native speaker would. Trip once and it is not ready.

## Flow parity

**Strict** everywhere, verse and chorus. ESL singers, writing-camp toplines, and a stadium of non-native listeners cannot absorb a drifting line. V2 line n sings V1 line n: same syllables, same stress slots, same ending. Chanson: syllable count is the poetic form itself, **strict** by tradition.

Vowel parity on the hold is mandatory. The big note keeps the same open vowel every return, through the key change too. Nothing here is **off** or **loose**. A melody-first topline hands you the slot count and sense bends to it.

Count each language by its own rules. English as written, elisions marked. French counts a final mute e before a consonant and drops it before a vowel or at the line end; in song the melody decides whether a final e sounds. Italian counts to the last stressed syllable with adjacent vowels merged across words. Drafts in French, German, Italian, or any non-English language need a competent speaker on the brief.

Trap: a key-change final chorus that adds words because the arrangement got bigger. The words stay. The key moves.

## POV & voice

One I, one you. Eurovision wants a person singing to a person about a feeling a continent shares: love, loss, defiance. A unity anthem needs a crowd that earned it. Schlager is warm direct address with a named reference, a street, a season, a brand of lemonade. Italo is a poster persona. Chanson is a dry adult observer who notices and does not lecture.

Do not invent German, French, Italian, or any line in a language the brief did not supply with a competent speaker. The agent writes the English portion or asks.

English stays plain, current, un-idiomatic in the good sense. A tourist fakes "la la la" with nothing under it, random Euro place names, and accents sprinkled on the page.

## Hook

Eurovision: one universal word as the title, one verb, heard once by a continent. It arrives early, repeats, and survives the lift. It is not a slogan, a list, or an idiom.

A schlager refrain is a toast or a wave the room can join. A Sanremo chorus is the melody's high point, text-forward. A chanson refrain, when there is one, is a returning image. A Scandinavian chorus lands inside about 50 seconds and is hummable before it is understood.

The title never needs a gloss. If a listener must translate it, the hook does not travel.

## Kill list

- "La la la" with nothing under it
- Random Euro place names as perfume
- Fake accents and diacritics on the page
- Unity anthem with no person: hands up, one world, one heart
- Broken-grand English: eternity, destiny, flames in my soul
- Schlager cruise-ship sunset as the fix for everything
- German, French, or Italian lines invented from vibes
- Title that needs a gloss
- Idiom or pun a non-native ear cannot catch
- Final chorus with extra words
- Chanson as rain, café, and cigarette with no street

## AI failure modes

Decorative multilingualism. Latinate English a second-language singer cannot place. Counts that drift between verses and between returns. A final chorus longer than the first because the arrangement lifted. A bridge that introduces a second idea in a three-minute song. Eurovision as a TED talk. Schlager cheer with no reference. Italo nonsense that is random words with no mouth logic. Chanson shaped like English pop. Universal love so universal it names nothing. Every section one density.

If a chorus changes shape between returns, or a non-native singer would trip on the first listen, it is not done.

## Briefing questions

1. Which lane exactly, and is the singer a native English speaker?
2. Is any of it non-English, and who is the competent speaker for those lines?
3. Does the last chorus lift by key change, by drop, or stay flat?

## Hybrid note

Primary when a sing-along across languages, melody-first fitting, or text-forward chanson outranks a native-English diary. `pop.md` donates its kill list so the chorus is not an empowerment list or a fireworks sky. `electronic.md` donates its kill list for Italo loops: do not novelize a loop. `east-asia.md` donates its kill list against unsingable clusters and broken-grand English. Do not average Eurovision's one-idea build with dance-pop's pre and post, and do not average chanson's stanza form with a folk diary (`folk.md`). Never hybrid into invented German, French, or Italian.
