# Brazil

The melody is already in the speech. Write to the stress, honour the refrain, let the room answer. Brochure Brazil is the failure.

## Sub-lanes

- **MPB / bossa nova** — text-forward. Melodic accents land on stressed syllables, delivery near a whisper, one note per syllable. Understatement: a small thing noticed says the big thing.
- **Samba / pagode** — the roda. A refrain the circle answers, verses that keep varying over a stable refrain. Table, not stage.
- **Sertanejo** — modão romantic and danceable, universitário, sofrência (romantic frustration and drinking). Duet trading lines.
- **Forró** — dance instruction, courtship, northeast place and work. Moves, keeps moving.
- **Funk carioca / baile funk** — chant hooks, call-and-response, MC verses. Lyrics get rewritten per community. Explicit body talk is native. The hook is the product.

If they want reggaeton, that is `latin.md`. Bossa written to a fixed standard's tune, AABA with changes, is `jazz.md`.

## Form

**Lane budget:** bossa and MPB 4–8 phrases per verse, refrain 2–4. Samba and pagode 2–6 varied verse phrases over a 2–4 phrase refrain. Sertanejo 4–6 per verse, chorus 2–6. Forró 2–6 per verse, refrain 1–4. Funk carioca chant 1–2 phrases, MC verse 4–8. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

Bossa and MPB: the melody usually leads and the words fit the tune. Short, closed, returning. V2 turns one small object, not the argument. A bridge is optional and often a mistake.

Samba and pagode run on the refrain. The leader keeps varying the verse; the circle answers the same words every time. V2 is the next improvisation: a new face, a new street, same answer. No pop bridge.

Sertanejo is verse and chorus built for two voices. Voice one calls, voice two answers, the chorus is where they land together. Sofrência verses prove the ache with a phone, a bar stool, an hour. V2 changes who is hurting or what time it is.

Forró keeps couplet-and-refrain pace and stays on its feet. Funk carioca writes the chant first, then leaves slots: a community will rewrite the verses, so the hook must survive that.

## Rhyme pressure

Medium, and lane-dependent. Portuguese hands you cheap chimes, verb endings rhyming with verb endings. Take them in a verse that is moving, never on the title or the end of a hook. Pay for end words that carry meaning.

Bossa and MPB can run soft assonance or none. The stress does the work. Sertanejo locks the chorus and makes the duet answer chime with the call. Pagode's refrain carries the rhyme and the verses vary around it. Funk carioca lets the chant outrank the scheme, with a hard end-hit.

Do not force English multi-rhyme culture onto Portuguese. If the page is English, do not sprinkle Portuguese chimes for garnish. Inverting speech to catch a rhyme is the tell, in either language.

## Pocket / mouth

Sung Portuguese keeps one note per syllable, which blocks the reductions a speaker makes mid-sentence. Write the syllable you want sung, not the one the mouth drops in talk.

Bossa is understatement at near-whisper. Low density, soft consonants, nothing pushed. Every word audible at conversation volume.

Samba and pagode are percussive: short words, a gap for the answer. Sertanejo puts long notes on the chorus vowels, and the duet's two lines are written to the same count so two throats land together. Forró stays dance-floor short and breathes on the step.

Funk carioca wants hard consonants, a chant a room of strangers can repeat on the first pass, and open slots for the next MC. Body talk is plain, not winked at.

Speak-test: bossa at a whisper, one syllable per tap of the hand. Funk, out loud, to a crowd. If a chant needs a breath in the middle, it is a verse.

## Flow parity

When a melody is supplied, it is law. Otherwise: bossa and MPB verses **tight**, because the melody usually leads and the words honour it, refrain **strict**. Sertanejo chorus **strict**, because two voices must land together, verses **tight**. Pagode refrain **strict**, verses **loose**. Funk carioca chant **strict**; MC verses **off** on syllables but not on bars: the end-hit lands on the same beat every bar.

The held vowel on a refrain or chorus keeps its family every return. Funk matches the hit, not the hold.

Portuguese counts to the last stressed syllable, like Spanish, and merges adjacent vowels across words (elisão). Drafts in Portuguese need a competent speaker on the brief. The agent writes the English portion or asks.

Trap: a sertanejo duet where the second voice's answer is a syllable longer than the first voice's call.

## POV & voice

Bossa is first person, a "you" in the room, an observation instead of a declaration. Samba's communal voice is earned by the roda; a leader sings neighbours and names. Sertanejo is first person to the ex, the barman, or the other voice, and a duet is two points of view, not one voice twice. Forró is couple, floor, work. Funk carioca is an MC in first person, body and block, rewritten by the community that sings it.

Regions are different mouths: Rio, São Paulo, the interior, the northeast. Pick the speaker's region and keep it. No phonetic dialect spelling.

Never invent Portuguese. A tourist fakes "saudade" as decoration, a carnival-and-beach brochure, the favela as costume, and Spanish words in a Portuguese lane. Sacred Afro-Brazilian material is not a costume rack.

## Hook

A bossa refrain is a small plain image or sentence, said as if overheard. A pagode refrain is the answer: short, singable by the circle on the first pass. A sertanejo chorus is a toast or a plea a bar can sing at closing time, both voices landing.

Funk carioca: the hook is the product. Chant and response, body first, short enough to shout, built to be rewritten around. Cleverness costs you the room.

A hook survives being answered, doubled, or rewritten. It is not a postcard and not a metaphor with a footnote.

## Kill list

- "Saudade" as decoration
- Carnival-and-beach brochure
- Favela as costume
- Spanish words in a Portuguese lane
- Bossa whispered with no observation under it
- Northeast as sepia: sun, dust, a sad accordion
- Sofrência with a bottle and no named hurt
- Pagode refrain nobody can answer
- Duet where both voices say the same thing
- Funk hook that needs its verse to make sense
- English pop idiom carried over word for word

## AI failure modes

Brochure Brazil: beach, carnival, moon, saudade. English counting on Portuguese lines. Speech reductions written into sung syllables. Bossa belted, samba without a refrain, pagode verses that repeat instead of varying, sertanejo duets where both voices are the same voice. Funk hooks over-explained, sanitized, or written as a verse. One "Brazilian" voice for five regions. Spanish bleed. Fake dialect spelling.

If it reads like a postcard sent from the country and not a note passed inside the room, it is not done.

## Hybrid note

Primary when lane, language of record, and the answer test (refrain, duet, or chant) lead. Bossa that leans on standard craft borrows the `jazz.md` kill list; if the tune is a fixed standard, `jazz.md` is primary. Sertanejo-pop: keep the duet landing and run the `pop.md` kill list against the empowerment chorus. Funk carioca with rap verses: the `hip-hop.md` kill list against empty brand drops and costume slang. Do not average a bossa whisper with an electronic loop, or a pagode refrain with an airy trap hook. Hybrid is not permission to fake Portuguese.
