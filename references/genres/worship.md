# Worship

A room sings it after one pass, to God, in plain speech, with the claim named. Not a love song with a capital letter. Not a lecture with a tune.

## Sub-lanes

- **Congregational worship** — sung by a room that has heard it once. Narrow range, about a tenth. Steps and small skips, one note per syllable, repeated lines. Vertical address to God in second person. A "we" earned by the room being present.
- **CCM radio** — Hot Christian AC and Christian CHR forms. Verse, pre, chorus, bridge. A personal "I" testimony. Radio hook rules apply.
- **Modern hymn** — strophic, text-dense, doctrine in plain speech. Each stanza a new facet of one idea. Refrain optional.
- **Bridge-loop worship** — a two- or four-line bridge that repeats and builds. The moment the room sings loudest.

Choir vamp, testimony, and altar call in the Black church tradition is `gospel.md`. A love song with Jesus swapped for baby is `pop.md` failing ethics.

## Form

**Room default:** congregational verses 4 phrases, chorus 2–4, bridge 2–4 that repeat; CCM verse 4–6, pre 2–4, chorus 2–6, bridge 2–4; modern hymn 3–5 stanzas of 4 lines, refrain 0–2. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

Congregational: verse, chorus, optional bridge. The chorus is the simplest thing in the song. The bridge is simpler still. A room learns the chorus on pass one and the bridge by pass three.

CCM: radio-sectional. Verse proves with one real scene, pre lifts, chorus claims, bridge turns toward the loop. The "I" holds from first line to last.

Modern hymn: strophic. Stanza one states the idea, every later stanza turns a new facet of it, and the last stanza lands the claim without tidying it. No refrain unless the brief wants one.

V2 adds a scene or a new facet, never a second slogan. Doctrine arrives as a named event or claim, not an adjective.

Bridge loop: the loudest moment is the plainest text. Same words every pass. The build lives in the room, not on the page.

## Rhyme pressure

Low to medium. Plain end words the room can catch on first hearing. Identical repeats are the device in a bridge loop.

Hymns rhyme like hymns: clean, simple, expected. Archaic inversion to land a chime is costume unless the brief's tradition actually sings it.

Do not bend scripture's English to rhyme. A worship line that reads like a puzzle gets sung wrong.

## Pocket / mouth

Write for a range of about a tenth: no word that wants a leap, no line that climbs two registers. One note per syllable, no melisma, no word the room has to decode while singing it.

The held note carries the name or a single open vowel. Short words on the lift. Consonant clusters choke a room; leave them for the verse.

Breath falls at the end of a phrase, where a mixed room breathes together.

Speak-test: sing the chorus flat, unaccompanied, to someone who has not heard it. If they cannot join by the second line, simplify. Read it once, close the page, repeat it. If you cannot, neither can the room.

## Flow parity

**Strict** everywhere, verses included. A congregation learns by repetition and cannot absorb a drifting line. Bridge loops are identical on every pass. One note per syllable, so syllable count is melody. The held vowel on the lift matches on every return; the full vowel parity in [../flow.md](../flow.md) applies to chorus and loop.

Modern hymn stanzas are **strict** to stanza one, line for line. It is the oldest parity tradition in English song.

Nothing here is **loose** or **off**. Use contractions and marked elisions to hold the count, not extra syllables.

Trap: a second verse that adds a theological qualifier and a syllable.

## POV & voice

Congregational worship is "we" and a "You" addressed upward. CCM radio is a personal "I". They are different pockets. Do not blur them in one song.

Name the specific claim. "Your hand is on me" fits any religion. "The grave is empty" fits one. Generic lyrics any faith could sing are the known failure. Vague "victory" with no object is the cliché. The other ditch is a doctrine lecture nobody can sing.

Theology is checked by the brief's tradition. The agent does not invent doctrine. Honour the user's denomination and vocabulary. If the tradition is not given, write only the claims the brief supplies, or ask.

No mocking, no costume piety. A sprinkle of archaic address is not reverence.

## Hook

One claim, one verb, in words a stranger can sing back. The chorus is the simplest section. The held note carries the name or the open vowel.

It survives a tired room, off-pitch, a line late. It does not list attributes, lecture, or flirt.

The bridge is simpler than the chorus. It repeats without changing a word. If the bridge needs new lyrics each pass, the loop is not a loop.

## Kill list

- "Victory" with no battle
- Fire, shadow, light, storm as the whole bank
- "Unfailing" and "endless" stacked as adjectives
- A chorus that lists attributes
- "This is the moment" brochure
- A bridge that changes words every repeat
- Latinate sermon diction in a sung line
- Love-song tropes with a capitalised You
- A tidy prosperity ending
- Lines any religion could sing
- Doctrine the brief did not supply

## AI failure modes

Generic spirituality with no named claim. Abstractions standing in for events. Attribute lists as choruses. Verses written as sermons. A love-song skeleton with God's name pasted in. "I" and "we" blurred in one song. A chorus too clever to learn in one pass. A bridge that changes every repeat. Held notes on closed vowels or clusters. Range-hopping lines. Fire, light, and storm as the whole vocabulary. Archaic address sprinkled for effect. A second verse longer than the first, with a qualifier. Invented denominational doctrine. A tidy ending that makes the faith a result.

If a stranger cannot sing the chorus back after one pass, or any faith could sing it, it is not done.

## Briefing questions

1. Congregational, CCM radio, modern hymn, or bridge loop?
2. Which tradition's vocabulary does the room sing in, and what is the one claim the song names?
3. Is the voice the room's "we" or one person's "I"?

## Hybrid note

Primary when a room must sing it after one pass, or when stated doctrine is the content. `gospel.md` donates its kill list when the praise tilts toward testimony or vamp (prosperity clichés, psalm-sticker mashups). `pop.md` donates its kill list for CCM radio so the chorus is not an empowerment list. Worship-gospel: do not average a congregation's one-pass chorus with a choir's eight-minute vamp. If the choir answers carry the song, `gospel.md` is primary. CCM with a congregational chorus: do not mix the "I" of testimony and the "we" of the room. Pick the pocket and hold it.
