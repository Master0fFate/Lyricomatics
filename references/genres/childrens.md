# Children's

The listener is four and the singer is tired. The lyric is a handle: one small thing a child can pick up, repeat, and do with the body. If they sing it they remember it, so the line that repeats must be worth remembering.

## Sub-lanes

- **Nursery / preschool** — tetrameter or trimeter, four-beat feel, trochaic lilt. Repetition is the teaching method. Call-and-response. Actions the body can do on the beat.
- **Educational** — one concept per song, said in several frames. Teach and be funny in the same line. The concept word lands on the strong beat and the rhyme.
- **Lullaby** — long open vowels, slow, few words. One safe image that does not change. Second person to the child. No plot.
- **Story song** — a character, a problem, a rule of three, a refrain the child can shout. The resolution is an action, not a lecture.
- **Family folk / camp** — zipper songs change one word per verse. Cumulative songs add one item per verse and repeat the whole list.

If they want a lullaby for an adult lover, that is `folk.md` or `rnb.md`. If they want a parody for grown-ups, that is `comedy.md`.

## Form

**Skeleton default:** nursery stanzas of 2–4 phrases; educational verses of 4–6 phrases around a 2-phrase refrain; lullaby 2–4 phrases per verse, 2–3 verses; story song 3–5 verses of 4 phrases plus a 1–2 phrase refrain; zipper and cumulative songs one 2–4 phrase frame, reused. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

The child learns the tune first and the words second. Draft verse 1 as a mold. Every later verse pours new content into the same shape: same line count, same job on the same beat.

Zipper songs change exactly one slot per verse and keep every other syllable. Cumulative songs add one item per verse, repeat the whole list, and keep the list order fixed. A reshuffled list is not variety. It is a broken song.

V2 changes the slot, never the shape. Story songs climb by threes: the first try fails, the second fails bigger, the third works and surprises. The refrain does not move.

No bridge. A bridge is a second tune to learn. A last verse that does the thing slowly, or a spoken tag, is the ending. Stop on the action. The lesson is what just happened.

## Rhyme pressure

High and plain. Perfect rhyme on words the child already owns. Slant rhyme teaches the ear a wrong answer.

The rhyme never needs a word the child does not know. If the chime wants *monsoon*, change the line. Educational lines put the concept word on the rhyme; its partner is a word the child owns, set up by the line before.

Speech order only. A child hears an inverted line as a mistake and keeps it. A mis-stressed word gets learned wrong. Invent your own pairs and games.

## Pocket / mouth

Vocabulary at the listener's level, plus one stretch word that the song teaches by context. One. The line around it shows what it means without defining it.

Every line sayable by a four-year-old without a cluster. No sixths, strengths, scripts, or *splashed streets*. Lean on *m, b, p, d, n, h* and open vowels. Short lines, one breath each: *Hop like a frog on the floor.* Three beats and a clap for the fourth.

Humor is physical and surprising. Something falls, sneezes, sits on the cat. Never ironic. The child does not hear irony and the adult who does has left the song. No fear imagery unless the song is about mastering it.

Say it at a four-year-old's pace, clapping the beat. Trip once, run out of air, or reach for a hard sound, and the line is not done. Then say it straight to a grown-up. If they wince, it is baby talk or condescension.

## Flow parity

**Strict** everywhere, verse and refrain, including **full vowel parity** on every stressed slot. The child learns the sound before the sense, so verse 3 is verse 1's exact skeleton with the new word in the same slot. Pickups only if every line has one. Endings match. The concept word sits on the same beat every verse.

Lullaby: **strict**, long vowels matched. The held slot keeps its family, and so does every other stressed slot.

Nothing here is **loose** or **off**. A zipper swap keeps the syllable count, the stress, and the vowel family of the word it replaces. Cast the verses by sound before you write them: *hen, pig, duck* share a family; *fox, frog, dog* share another. A cumulative list is a template, so each new item enters at the same position every time.

Trap: a verse that adds a syllable to fit the fourth animal's name. Change the animal, not the tune.

## POV & voice

A warm voice talking to the child, or the child's own first person, a voice they can borrow. Lullaby: second person, no plot. Story song: third person with a character a child could be. Camp song: *we*, only when the whole circle does the motion.

Speech a child hears at home. Nouns the child can point at, verbs the body can do. Kindness, friendship, and sharing are abstract until someone hands over a spoon. Write the spoon.

No baby-talk spelling, no cooing, no *good job!* tone. Adults in the room should not wince. Respect is the register.

The writer invents its own rhymes and games. Never quote or lightly rewrite a traditional rhyme or a known kids' TV song. A familiar skeleton with new nouns counts. If the brief gives a child's name, use it once, in the slot a name takes.

## Hook

The refrain a child can shout, clap, or do. It is what they will say to a grandparent at dinner, so it carries the fact, the move, or the name. Not a slogan.

It must survive thirty passes in a car, a flat voice, and a listener who learned it from one hearing. Short, one skeleton on every return, the concept word on the same beat.

It must not be a moral, a pep line, or nonsense that earns nothing. A sound the body makes is fine when every verse gives it a reason.

## Kill list

- Baby-talk spellings
- "Learning is fun" as a line
- A moral stated at the end
- Kindness or friendship as a noun with no action
- Three concepts in one song
- A rhyme that needs a word the child does not know
- Consonant clusters on the chant
- Irony aimed over the child's head
- A verse that changes shape
- Borrowed nursery-rhyme skeletons with new nouns
- Fear imagery with no mastery

## AI failure modes

Verse 3 grows because the story got interesting. The fourth animal breaks the meter. Vocabulary pitched at the parent. The concept announced instead of done. A moral coda. Rhymes lifted from tradition so the song feels familiar. Cuteness spelled instead of written. Adjectives stacked where the child needs a verb. Three concepts crowding one refrain. Fear brought in for texture and never beaten. A refrain that restates the title in new adjectives.

If a four-year-old cannot sing verse 3 after hearing verse 1 once, it is not done.

## Briefing questions

1. Which lane, and how old is the listener?
2. What is the one thing the child can say or do when the song ends?
3. What does the body do on the beat, or for a lullaby, which one safe image stays still?

## Hybrid note

Primary whenever the listener is a child and the song is built to be learned by ear. Camp and family folk: `folk.md` donates its kill list so plain does not turn sentimental. Kid-pop: `pop.md` kill list, so the refrain is not an empowerment bullet list for a four-year-old. Funny songs: `comedy.md` donates punch placement, not its irony. Do not average a lullaby with an adult torch song: one is for sleep, the other is for the singer. Do not average an educational song with satire. The child cannot hear the target.
