# Jazz

Adult motives, double meanings, scansion as law. Wit is a temperature, not a costume with a saxophone in it. The tune’s bones boss the syllable count.

## Sub-lanes

- **Swing / standard lyric** — sectional return (often AABA or similar). Title phrase lands on the musical title motif. Internal elegance, clean talk.
- **Ballad / torch** — softer rhyme, longer vowels, late-night address. Still scanned. Sentiment without a concrete is perfume.
- **Vocalese** — words mapped to an existing instrumental melody. Hard constraint. Do not attempt without a syllable-to-note plan. Scat on the page is phonetic play only if the brief wants it notated; otherwise performance, not lyric.

If there is no fixed tune and they want diary shame, you probably wanted `folk.md`. If they want theatre objective in a room, `theatre.md`.

## Form

Respect sectional return. A sections reprise with meaning that can tilt; they are not copy-paste. The bridge (B) is a true turn of thought or address, not a prettier A.

Do not write through-composed pop with jazz adjectives. If the brief names AABA (or another standard shape), the lyric must occupy those rooms.

Vocalese: map stresses to the target melody before you fall in love with a sentence. If the sentence will not fit, the sentence loses.

Repetition of a title motif is branding, not fear, when it lands where the tune lands.

## Rhyme pressure

Elegant. Internals welcome. Witty ends welcome in swing. Ballad rhyme softer, still intentional.

Perfect rhyme is a tool of poise, not a god. Forced nursery rhyme on a torch song is death.

Do not rhyme-first invert into unspeakable cabaret Yoda. These singers still have to act the line.

End word is the meaning word — often the turn of the joke or the knife of the address.

## Pocket / mouth

Melody is tyrant. Syllable count and stress must fit the bones. Melisma is the singer’s greed, not the lyricist’s. Do not stack extra syllables because the sentence was pretty on paper.

Speak it in time. If you cannot, it will not sit. Over-syllabic lines are the classic failure.

Open vowels on long tones. Consonant play on faster passing tones. Do not park a cluster on a held note and hope the singer is a magician.

Scatting is not your alibi for a weak lyric.

## POV & voice

Sophisticated first or second. Urbane observation. Late-night address. Adult motives: pride, bargaining, boredom, lust, money, cowardice, charm.

Double meanings beat single-entendre “jazziness.” Do not name-drop instruments, clubs-as-stickers, or “city lights” postcards as personality.

Forced hipster slang is worse than plain speech. Wit level is a briefing dial: dry, romantic, or biting. Pick and hold.

Period diction if the brief sets an era. Do not mix 2020s app-talk into a 1940s torch unless CAST is doing irony on purpose.

## Hook

Title phrase on the musical title motif. A turn of phrase you could quote at a table.

The hook can be a shrug. It cannot be a motivational poster in a tuxedo.

One surprising concrete — a coat, a tab, a name, a time — keeps the sophistication from floating away.

## Kill list

- Noir costume without a detail
- “City lights” postcard
- Forced hipster slang
- Ignoring scansion for “poetry”
- Instrument name-drops as personality
- Vocalese without a syllable-to-note map
- Purple adjective weather
- Over-syllabic lines that fight the tune
- Therapy nouns in a torch mouth
- Writing “jazzy” instead of writing motives

## AI failure modes

Purple. Unscanned. Saxophone mentions. Too many syllables. Fake slang. Mood-board noir. Ballad that is only perfume. Swing lyric with no joke and no knife. Standard form ignored. Vocalese as a poem taped onto a solo.

If the line would work identically over a pop ballad and a blues, you have not occupied this pocket.

## Briefing questions

1. Is there a fixed melody or form (AABA or other) the lyric must occupy?
2. Ballad or swing attitude?
3. Wit level — dry, romantic, or biting?
