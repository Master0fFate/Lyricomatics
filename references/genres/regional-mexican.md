# Regional Mexican

A chronicle, a toast, or a dare, said by someone who could be sitting at the table. The forms are old and the audience checks papers. Costume dies in stanza one.

## Sub-lanes

- **Corrido** — classic and narrative ballad. Event, name, place, consequence. One fact per line. The story is the hook.
- **Banda / norteño romántico** — ballad, sad love, drinking song. Partner search, daily life, desire, separation. The glass is on the table and somebody left.
- **Sierreño** — acoustic requinto, voice close to the string. Few words, small room, nowhere to hide.
- **Tumbados / bélicos** — trap-style delivery and auto-tune over norteño and banda instruments. Tumbados run laid-back or street; bélicos stress courage and indifference to death. Coded insider vocabulary is the currency.

If they want reggaeton or salsa, that is `latin.md`. If it is Brazilian Portuguese, that is `brazil.md`.

## Form

**Chronicle default:** corrido runs 3–6 quatrains, longer only when each stanza carries a new fact, with 0–2 refrain lines at most. Banda and norteño ballads take 4–6 phrases per verse and 2–4 in a chorus. Sierreño verses sit at 4–6, refrain short. Tumbados verses run 8–16 bars, hook 2–4. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

Classic corrido is strophic: octosyllabic quatrains on one tune, usually in 3/4, two or three chords. The tune never changes. The facts do. Open with the formal frame, date and place and who. Close on the despedida, the farewell stanza where the singer takes leave. Skip both and it is a poem with a guitar.

Ballads are verse and chorus. Verses prove the loss, the chorus is the verdict or the toast. V2 shifts the hour, the glass, or who left. Bridge is rare. A stripped last chorus or a final verse that changes what we know does more.

Sierreño wants one image per stanza and no pile-up. Tumbados want a stance in the verse and a short stamp in the hook. V2 changes the room or the debt, not the length of the brand list.

## Rhyme pressure

Medium-high, and it is assonance. A corrido rhymes lines 2 and 4 on the vowels from the last stressed vowel on; consonants are free. ABCB in practice, lines 1 and 3 loose. Do not tidy it into AABB.

Ballads chime harder in the chorus and relax in the verse. End words are table words, plain enough to say over noise. Tumbados thin the scheme: mood, a name, one flex noun on the beat. Thin is not empty.

English-language corrido-style chronicles cannot borrow Spanish assonance. Slant-rhyme lines 2 and 4, keep the stanza skeleton, stop there. No Spanish chimes dropped in for garnish. Inverting speech to catch a vowel is the tell. The narrator tells it straight.

## Pocket / mouth

A corrido is told, not performed. Plain words, one fact per line, names a listener catches the first time. Leave a breath at stanza end. The instrument answers there.

Banda and norteño ballads sustain. The chorus rides long notes on open vowels and the title sits on one. Do not cram a verse with clauses and ask the singer to sob through them.

Sierreño sits near speech. Close to the string means low density, soft attack, no belt. Write what you could say across a small table.

Tumbados borrow trap air. Short words, room for the auto-tuned note to bend, gaps on purpose. A name list is a rhythm, so every item is a word this narrator owns.

Speak-test: say it at table volume with no music. If it sounds like a news item or a movie trailer, rewrite it.

## Flow parity

Classic corrido **strict**: eight counted syllables per line is the form itself, stanza to stanza. Banda and sierreño ballad chorus **strict**, verses **tight**. Tumbados verses are **loose** inside a stanza and **tight** stanza to stanza: stanza 2 line n still sings stanza 1 line n. Tumbado hook **strict**. Nothing here is **off**.

The held vowel on the last stressed syllable of a chorus line keeps its family every return. In a corrido the assonant vowel on lines 2 and 4 is the cadence. Do not swap it mid-song for a prettier word.

Spanish counts to the last stressed syllable. A line ending on a stressed syllable adds one; a line ending on a word stressed on the antepenult subtracts one. Adjacent vowels merge across word boundaries (sinalefa). Count in Spanish when the lyric is Spanish. English chronicles count English syllables and keep the stanza skeleton.

Trap: a tumbado flex that grows a syllable per line as the brand list grows. Cut an item. Do not stretch the bar.

## POV & voice

Corrido is third-person chronicle, a narrator reporting, or a first-person claim by the subject. Pick one and hold it through the despedida. Ballads are first person to the one who left, or to the bottle. Sierreño is a confession kept low. Tumbados are first person, insider, present tense.

Drafts in Spanish need a competent speaker on the brief. The agent writes the English portion or asks. Never invent Spanish.

Coded cartel and street vocabulary marks who lives it. Comprehension is the credential, and fans punish writers who borrow the slang without the life. If the brief did not supply the words, write plain and say so in CAST.

No crime glamour unless the brief owns that conversation. If it does, no cartoon blood, no unearned names, no real living person without the brief's say. A tourist fakes "mi gente", random Spanish nouns, and narco signifiers.

## Hook

A corrido has no hook in the radio sense. The frame is the hook: the opening formula, the returning tune, the despedida. The title names the person, the place, or the event.

A ballad chorus is a toast or a verdict a full table can sing back after midnight. Plain words on an open vowel. Cleverness goes in the verse. A sierreño refrain is one image, said soft, the same every return.

A tumbado hook is short and repeatable and carries a stance, a name, or a number. It survives auto-tune and a bad speaker. It is not a brand inventory, and the flex or the dread gets one verb, not both.

## Kill list

- Corrido with no date, place, or name in the opening
- Despedida that moralizes, or goes missing
- Crime glamour with no funeral, no bill, no mother
- "Mi gente" and random Spanish nouns in an English song
- Pickups, rifles, and sierra sunrise as the whole personality
- Coded slang borrowed without the life behind it
- Bélico courage with no cost
- Heartbreak-and-tequila with no glass, table, or hour
- Tumbado hook that is a brand list
- Filler vowels to pad a line to eight
- Tidy AABB perfect rhyme on a corrido

## AI failure modes

Chronicle with no facts: a hero, a sunrise, a bad end. Corrido written as a poem with no frame, no despedida, and a chorus it never had. English syllable counting on a Spanish line. Perfect AABB where assonance on lines 2 and 4 was the form. Narco aesthetics as default wallpaper. Tumbado slang pasted from memory, stacked from three regions. Ballads that sob in essay sentences. Sierreño belted like banda. Every stanza one density. A moral tied on the last stanza. Invented Spanish dressed as dialect.

If a listener from the town in the song could not name who, where, and what was lost by the second stanza, it is not done.

## Briefing questions

1. Which lane exactly, and is the lyric Spanish, English, or split?
2. Who is the subject, what happened, and is the name real or invented?
3. Who on the brief is competent in the vocabulary, and where are the violence lines?

Primary when lane, language of record, and the who-where-when test lead. `latin.md` donates its narco-cosplay and decorative-Spanish kills, and its ethics stay law. Tumbados: this file owns vocabulary and ethics, `hip-hop.md` donates its kill list (crown, empty brand drops, cost-free flex), still one primary. Never costume tumbado slang and drill slang in one mouth. Hybrid is not permission to fake fluency.
