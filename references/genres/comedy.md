# Comedy

The joke is the last word. Everything before it is setup, and the setup's job is to make the punch the only surprise. The ear should see the slot coming and still lose to the word.

## Sub-lanes

- **Parody** — match the source's syllable count, stress, cadence, and inflection line for line. That is the entire craft. The joke is the gap between the kept shape and the new sense. The original's rhyme position becomes the punch slot.
- **Novelty / comic song** — a premise taken seriously by a narrator who does not know it is funny. Escalation by verse. Rule of three, third item breaks the pattern. The setup line is plain so the punch is the only surprise.
- **Musical comedy / patter** — character wit at the character's intelligence and class. List songs that escalate. Internal rhyme as a weapon. The last item recasts the list.
- **Comedy rap / sketch song** — deadpan delivery of an absurd premise. The beat is straight, so the words are the joke. Callbacks to a throwaway line. The hook is the premise stated flat.

If they want a theatre number whose first job is plot, that is `theatre.md`. If they want a kids' funny song, that is `childrens.md`. If they want satire with an enemy and a shout, that is `punk.md`.

## Form

**Premise default:** novelty verses of 4–8 phrases, setup first and punch last; hook 2–4 phrases; patter stanzas of 4–8 lines; comedy rap 8–16 bars with a 2–4 bar hook; parody follows the source's phrase map exactly. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

State the premise flat before you write a line: one sentence, no joke in it. A narrator takes it seriously. If the sentence is not funny plain, the verses are decoration.

Build every verse from setup and punch pairs. The setup is plain speech. The punch lands on the rhyme. Lists run two items to set the pattern and a third that breaks it.

V2 escalates: higher stakes or deeper absurdity, not more adjectives. The same joke in verse two is a repeat, not a build. Plant a throwaway line early, a small odd fact in verse one, and pay it back once, late.

No sincere bridge. A bridge, if any, breaks the pattern or cashes the callback. End on the punch. No outro that explains it.

## Rhyme pressure

High, clean, and placed. Perfect rhyme where the punch lives. The setup's rhyme word can be the plainest word on the page; its job is to make the ear lean forward.

A rhyme that needs a mis-stress is a joke on the writer. Do not invert speech to catch the chime. Inversion buries the punch and the audience hears the effort.

One rhyme can arrive too elegantly for an ugly premise. That mismatch is a tool: a graceful chime on a grim fact beats a clumsy one. Spend it once per song.

Working method: list candidate rhymes sorted by syllable count, one, two, three, then pick the count that fits the slot. Puns need a premise to stand on. Stacked puns with none are noise.

## Pocket / mouth

The audience must hear the setup clearly. Setup lines are low-density plain speech: short words, no clever clause competing with the plant. Spend density on the punch line, not before it.

The punch word is the end word and the meaning word. Never mid-line, never trailed by adjectives. A joke that needs a second line to explain it is dead.

The singer must deliver it deadpan without tripping. No clusters on the punch, no tongue-twister unless the twister is the premise. Leave a half-beat of air before the punch word. The breath is part of the joke.

Patter earns density, and every word still lands. Internal rhyme loads the ear so the end word fires. It is not decoration for the middle of the bar.

Speak it flat to one friend, no tune, no face. If you smile, rush the last word, or reach for a gesture to sell it, rewrite.

## Flow parity

**Strict** on setup and punch pairs across verses, so the audience's ear predicts the slot and the word surprises them in it. A hook that is the premise stated flat returns **strict**.

Parody is **strict** to the source map the user supplied, syllable for syllable, with the stressed vowel of the punch word matched to the setup's rhyme vowel. List songs are **strict** item to item except the last item, which may run long once for the joke. Comedy rap verses are **off** on syllables because flow is the design. Bar parity still holds and the punch sits on the same beat each bar.

Stress correctness outranks the joke. A funny word on the wrong beat is a dropped joke.

Trap: a punchline that needs one more syllable than the slot has, so the singer rushes the only word that matters. Trim a word upstream.

## POV & voice

First person for the narrator who does not know. Third person for fable or reportage told straight. Second person only when the audience shares the embarrassment. The narrator commits. A wink says the writer does not trust the premise.

Character wit sits at the character's intelligence and class. A plumber and a professor do not share a pun. If everyone in the song is equally quick, nobody is the fool.

Aim at the narrator, an institution, an object, a habit. Meanness at a real private person is banned. Public figures are fair only for public acts, with no invented facts. Accent and dialect are not the joke.

Parody of a real song is a legal and ethical question for the user. The writer does not reproduce the original's lyrics and does not write parody of a specific real song unless the user supplies the target's syllable map themselves. Even then, only the new words go on the page.

## Hook

The premise stated flat, as a title or refrain. If the hook is only a funny phrase, there is nothing under it for the verses to escalate.

It must survive a deadpan read by a singer who is not a comedian. It must get funnier on the third return because the verses changed what it means. The words stay identical; the meaning moves.

It must not be a pun, a catchphrase, or a noise standing in for an idea. A title that announces the joke spends it.

## Kill list

- Explaining the joke in the next line
- A punch buried mid-line
- Random words as humor
- Puns stacked with no premise
- The narrator winking
- A sincere bridge
- The same joke in verse two
- Rhymes that need a mis-stress
- A hook that is only a funny phrase and not a premise
- Meanness at a real private person
- Parody that reprints the source's lines with nouns swapped

## AI failure modes

Jokes explained one line later. Punch in the middle with a tail of adjectives after it. Setup lines so clever the plant is missed. Random objects offered as comedy. Puns with no situation. Three verses of one joke. Escalation by adjective. A narrator who winks, or turns sincere at the end to prove the writer has a heart. Callbacks that were never planted. Rhymes that mis-stress to hit the punch. Every character equally witty. Parody that quietly reproduces the source.

If the singer has to sell the punch with a face, a pause, or a wink, it is not done.

## Briefing questions

1. What is the premise in one flat sentence, and who takes it seriously?
2. Parody, novelty, patter, or rap, and for parody can you supply the target's syllable and stress map yourself?
3. Who is the fool of the song, and who must it never touch?

## Hybrid note

Primary when the laugh is the job and the punch slot is the structure. Character-voice comedy: `theatre.md` donates its kill list so the wit stays at the character's class, and if the number's first job is plot, theatre is primary. Comedy rap: `hip-hop.md` kill list for rhyme-bossed syntax; the punch stays here. Comic pop or country: `pop.md` or `country.md` kill list, so the hook is not a slogan and the dialect is not a costume. A kids' funny song is `childrens.md` primary, with this kill list for punch placement. Do not average comedy with a sincere ballad: a song with one joke and a real ache is `folk.md` or `country.md`. Do not average it with satire that has an enemy and a shout. That is `punk.md`.
