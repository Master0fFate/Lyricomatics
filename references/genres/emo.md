# Emo

A confession a room can sing back. The verse is the diary; the chorus is the one plain line everybody gets to own. Specific enough to cost something, simple enough to shout.

## Sub-lanes

- **Emo** — confessional, imagist, metaphor-cloaked. Breathy head voice, not punk shout. Free-associative honesty that still names the car.
- **Pop-punk** — classic pop songcraft under distortion. Verse, pre, big chorus, gang vocals, whoa-oh chants, a fast count-off. Lust, heartbreak, suburbia, leaving town.
- **Screamo and post-hardcore** — clean/scream splits. The scream carries the line the clean voice cannot say. Breath groups are blows.
- **Midwest emo** — talk-sung verses, twinkly guitars, long ironic titles that are a joke the song then refuses to tell. Diary dates, basements, driving home.

If they want slogan and enemy with no confession, that is `punk.md`. Band throat and a shirt-print chorus with no diary is `rock.md`; an acoustic diary with no distortion is `folk.md`.

## Form

**Singalong default:** 4–6 sung phrases per verse (midwest talk-verses up to 8), 2–4 in a pre, 2–4 in a chorus, 0–2 in a post or tag; screamed sections run 2–4 chunks. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

Verse → pre → chorus is native to pop-punk. Emo and midwest can skip the pre and let the verse fall into the chorus. Either way the chorus is simpler than the verse. If the room cannot learn it on the first pass, it cannot sing it back on the second.

The pre often withholds the one specific confession: what was said in the car, whose name was on the phone. The chorus does not repeat it. The chorus reacts to it, in plain words.

The long title is a frame, not the hook. *The Garage Door Was Open Again at 4 a.m.* sets the room; the chorus line is shorter and lives somewhere else. Land the title once, whole. Do not let it be the only joke in the song.

V2 is later in the night, or after the breakup. New room, worse decision, different phone. The same scene re-adjectived is dead weight. Screamo splits the work: the clean verse says what happened, the scream section says what it did.

Bridge is a drop to one voice, a time jump, or the scream section itself. A bridge that reads a diary entry aloud stalls the song. Repeat the chant and the chorus; let the verses move.

## Rhyme pressure

Medium on the chorus, low to medium in verses. Pop-punk wants clean ends on the hook. Emo verses live on slant and image ends. Midwest talk-verses can go unrhymed if the line ending is matched.

*Forever* is the rhyme crutch of the whole family. If the word arrived because of the chime, cut it.

Screamed sections rhyme by consonant, not vowel. Same hard end on the blow, or no rhyme at all.

Inversion gives it away at once. Nobody confesses in a car with "gone is the summer, lost is your name." Speech order, then find the stress.

## Pocket / mouth

Chorus: open vowel on the big held note, short words, no clusters. A whoa-oh is architecture, not filler. It is written, counted, and placed. Gang vocals need a line a drunk room can shape on one hearing.

Emo verses: breathy head voice, soft consonants are fine. Midwest talk-sung: the ugly stress of a person in a driveway beats the pretty stress of a caption. Dates and asides may run.

Screamed sections: syllables thin, consonants harden. A blow wants a stop consonant and one vowel. Write the chunk short enough to scream on one breath.

Speak test: whisper the verse into a pillow, shout the chorus across a parking lot. If the verse dies whispered or the chorus dies shouted, rewrite.

## Flow parity

Chorus **strict**: gang vocal, whoa-oh count exact every return, held vowel matched on the big note. Pop-punk verses **tight**. Midwest emo talk-verses **loose**, with the stress count held and the line ending matched. Screamed sections **loose** on syllables and **strict** on blow count per chunk.

The long title line, wherever it lands, keeps one skeleton on every return. If it opens V1 and closes the bridge, the two must sing alike.

Nothing here runs **off**. A talk-sung line is paced by speech and a scream thins under pressure, so syllables float; the stress count and the blows are what the room hears.

Trap: a whoa-oh chant that is four whoas in chorus one and five in chorus two.

## POV & voice

First person to a "you" with a name, or to the narrator at a date. Second-person accusation needs behavior: what you did, and when. Collective "we" only when the gang vocal earned it, a room that was all in that parking lot.

Register is nineteen at 2 a.m., not a counselor. Specificity is place, car, name, time, what the hand did: a thumb over send, a palm on the gearshift. Pop-punk suburbia is a real suburb. Name the strip mall.

Metaphor-cloaked emo holds one metaphor and lets it change state. Stacked metaphors are a mood board. Therapy vocabulary (boundaries, toxic, healing) in a basement mouth is a costume.

## Hook

The hook is the line the room owns. It states the cost of the confession in plain words, and it is simpler than anything in the verse. It must survive a gang vocal, a bad PA, and someone at the back screaming it wrong.

It must not be the long title, a mood label, or a diary entry. A whoa-oh is a hook only if it is counted. In screamo the hook may be the clean line that returns after the scream, or the scream chunk itself.

## Kill list

- Bleeding hearts
- Scars as currency
- "This town" with no town
- "You'll be sorry" with no behavior
- Rain on the window
- *Forever* as a rhyme crutch
- A bridge that is a diary entry read aloud
- Every section at the same volume on the page
- Therapy vocabulary in a basement mouth
- Whoa-oh filler where the chorus ran out of line

## AI failure modes

Universal sadness in a hoodie. Feelings named, nothing the hand did. A pre that gives the confession away so the chorus has nothing left to learn. A chorus longer than the verse. A quirky long title the lyric never touches, or one it explains. Gang lines that change shape on return. Scream sections written as paragraphs. V2 as the same scene re-adjectived. A town, a car, and a name that could belong to anyone's ex. Every section pitched at one level of drama.

If a stranger cannot shout the chorus back after one pass, or the verse never names a street, a car, or what a hand did, it is not done.

## Briefing questions

1. Which lane, and who carries the chorus: one voice, a gang, or a clean/scream split?
2. What is the one specific thing the narrator never said, and which section withholds it?
3. Where and when is V1 (town, car, hour), and what hour is V2?

## Hybrid note

Primary when a diary confession and a chorus the room sings back lead. `punk.md` donates its kill list (rebellion with no target, thesaurus emo) so grievance does not turn to karaoke; `rock.md` donates aesthetic adjective fog; `pop.md` donates the chorus-as-list-of-feelings ban when the lane is pop-punk. Pop-punk with no confession, only a cute belted claim, is `pop.md` primary; a wall-slogan chorus with a named enemy is `punk.md` primary. Metalcore: if the scream carries doctrine or a held metaphor domain, `metal.md`; if it carries a diary line, stay here. Do not average a smudged vowel-wash with a shoutable chorus. Pick which one the room remembers.
