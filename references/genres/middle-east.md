# Middle East

Name the language and the dialect before the first line. The refrain is the song; the verse leaves room for the singer's ornament. Drafts in Arabic, Turkish, or Persian need a competent speaker on the brief.

## Sub-lanes

- **Arabic pop** — Egyptian colloquial is the default lyric register of pop across the region. Khaleeji pop blends Gulf rhythm with pop melody and sometimes simplifies poetic language for a wider audience. Lebanese pop sings in Lebanese; do not hand it Egyptian by default. The refrain repeats heavily; the verse carries ornament space.
- **Turkish pop and arabesk** — arabesk: melancholy, fate, longing, the city's cruelty, and a suspicion that it lost its protest edge. Turkish pop: direct address and refrain.
- **Persian pop** — poetic register, love and exile, classical imagery kept alive in pop diction.
- **Arabic trap and rap** — dialect-specific: Darija, Egyptian, Levantine. Flow over sparse beats, flex with local economics.
- **Mawwal / layali** — a solo sung improvisation on a colloquial poem that opens a song, unmetered. The Baghdadi mawwal runs seven lines, AAABBBA. The layali is its unmetered vocal counterpart. The singer improvises both; a lyricist does not write them.

If the bars are the whole product with no dialect frame, that is `hip-hop.md`. If the brief is devotional or liturgical text, stop and ask. This is not a pop lane.

## Form

**Refrain default:** pop verses 4–6 sung phrases, refrain 2–4, pre optional at 2–3. A mawwal opener is a slot, not a section. Arabic trap verses 8–16 bars, hook 2–4. Fewer syllables per phrase than an English pop line of the same length. See [../economy.md](../economy.md).

Refrain-led. Verse, refrain, verse, refrain. The refrain states the song in a line the room sings back. The verse sets the ache or the dare and leaves the singer room to ornament.

V2 moves the grievance: a later hour, a new accusation, the phone in another hand. Arabesk does not heal by the last refrain; it takes more. No bridge by default. A change of addressee, from the beloved to the city, does a bridge's work.

Repetition is the design. The refrain returns word for word. Do not pad it with new clauses to feel written. If a return changes, the music asked.

A mawwal opener goes in CAST as a slot. The singer improvises on a colloquial poem. Supply poem text only if a competent poet wrote it; otherwise leave the slot marked and open.

## Rhyme pressure

Medium on the refrain, light on pop verses, higher where the lane prizes poetic diction: Persian pop, arabesk. The mawwal's AAABBBA is a poet's job, not a lyricist's.

Rhyme is dialect-bound. A chime in one pronunciation is not a chime in another; what lands in Egyptian can fail in Darija. Turkish and Persian suffix endings rhyme almost for free, and the free chime shows. Rhyme on the meaning word.

A formal-register word pulled in to catch a chime is the tell. Speech order, for the person in front of you.

## Pocket / mouth

Arabic vowels differ in length. The held note goes on a long vowel, and the ornament lives there; the singer owns it. So the page carries fewer syllables than an English pop line of the same length. Pack the verse and there is no vowel left to bend.

Leave long vowels open at line ends and at the refrain's landing. Never park a held note on a short one.

Turkish and Persian: the speaker marks where the line breathes. The agent keeps the English portion short and the slot clear.

Arabic trap: sparse beat, so leave air. A bar that doubles its load is a flow switch; name it in CAST.

Speak the English portion at tempo, then again with the ornament gaps left in. If the gaps vanish, you overwrote.

## Flow parity

Refrain **strict** on every return, with the held long vowel matched. Verses **tight**. Mawwal and layali **off**: improvised, unmetered, paired with nothing, and not the lyricist's to write beyond a poem text.

Arabic trap verses are **off** on syllables but owe bar parity: the rhyme lands on the same beat inside one flow pattern. Hook **strict**.

Count in the sung language, by weight. A long vowel is the slot the singer will hold; a short one is not. Do not scan Arabic, Turkish, or Persian with English stress. The speaker supplies the scansion, or the slot stays marked.

Trap: a refrain whose second return adds a word because the English translation needed it.

## POV & voice

Pop speaks to a beloved, a rival, or the city. Colloquial Egyptian is the language of pop and confession. Modern Standard Arabic is news and literature, and it sounds stiff in a love song. Do not mix dialects in one mouth.

Arabic marks the gender of "you" in the verb, so the brief must say who is addressed. Turkish and Persian do not, so there it can stay open.

The agent does not invent Arabic, Turkish, Persian, or dialect lines without a competent speaker on the brief. It writes the English portion, marks slots, or asks. A slot carries its syllable count, its held vowel, and its job.

A tourist fakes "habibi" as a hook, desert-and-oud imagery, "exotic" scale talk, and Turkish and Arabic words in one line. Arabesk fate and Persian exile live in particulars: a bus fare, a phone, a year.

## Hook

The refrain. Short enough to sing back after one pass, in one language, with a long vowel at the landing. It carries the ache or the dare. The verse carries the ornament.

It must survive heavy repetition and leave the singer room to ornament. Arabic trap hook: chantable, plain, true to the dialect of the city.

It must not be one address word, an English hook with a decorative Arabic word on top, or a translation of an English hook. Where the refrain is native, the speaker writes it. The agent specs the slot.

## Kill list

- "Habibi" as the whole hook
- Desert, moon, and oud as the whole set
- "Exotic" and "mystical East" as adjectives
- Turkish and Arabic words in one line
- Standard Arabic in a love confession, or two dialects in one mouth
- Arabesk fate with no city, no bill, no bus
- Rumi-poster aphorisms standing in for Persian diction
- Exile as weather: no phone, no airport, no year
- A refrain translated from an English hook
- A mawwal written out as metered verse
- Trap flex lifted from US vocabulary with no local currency, rent, or taxi

## AI failure modes

Exotic wallpaper in place of a person. Phrasebook Arabic, Turkish, or Persian with the wrong dialect, register, or gender of "you". Standard Arabic in a confession. Two dialects in one mouth. Turkish and Arabic mashed for flavor. A hook that is one address word. A verse so packed the singer has no long vowel to ornament. A refrain drafted in English, translated, then expanded on return. Fate and longing in the abstract. Rumi captions standing in for Persian diction. A mawwal scored as a metered verse. Arabic trap that is US flex vocabulary in a dialect costume.

If someone from the singer's city would ask who talks like that, it is not done.

## Briefing questions

1. Which language and dialect, which city, and who is the competent speaker for the native lines?
2. Who is speaking to whom, and is it confession, taunt, or flex?
3. Is there a mawwal or layali opener, and is the refrain native, English, or split?

## Hybrid note

Primary when dialect, a refrain-led shape, and ornament space lead. `pop.md` donates its kill list so the refrain is not an empowerment slogan; `hip-hop.md` donates for Arabic trap bars. Where bars are the product, `hip-hop.md` is primary and this file's language rules stay law. Khaleeji pop with a trap verse: this file owns the refrain and the dialect, `hip-hop.md` the verse pressure locally; one dialect throughout. Do not average an arabesk ache with a pop pre-chorus lift: arabesk takes more, the pre withholds, pick one. Never use a hybrid as permission to fake fluency.
