# UK Rap

Bars built for a room that talks back. Radio set, clash, bus-stop cipher: the line lands before somebody shouts for the rewind, and every word comes out of a London mouth.

## Sub-lanes

- **Grime** — a fast 140 feel, eight- and sixteen-bar patterns, built for the radio set and the clash. Punch early and often. Flow switches inside one verse are the skill on show. Entering a bar after a beat of silence is a device.
- **UK drill** — a 138–151 feel, sliding bass, a 3+3+2 skip in the hats, the snare on four every two bars. Intricate flows, not Chicago or Brooklyn's stream-of-consciousness aggression. British slang, threat as climate. Short lethal couplets, chant hook.
- **Garage / jungle MC** — hosting, not narrating. Ride the riddim from double time to singing, patois and street slang, crowd cues, the call for a rewind. The job is the room's energy.
- **Road rap / afroswing** — melodic, patois-inflected hooks, flex priced in London money.

If they want US-rooted flows and slang, that is `hip-hop.md`. A sung Afrobeats hook with a rap verse is `afro.md` primary. Dancehall toasting is `caribbean.md`.

## Form

**Pattern default:** grime verses 8 or 16 bars with a 2–4 bar hook or tag; drill 16 bars with a 4–8 bar chant; MC hosting 2–4 bar chants that repeat; afroswing 8–12 bar verses with a 4–8 bar melodic hook. See [../economy.md](../economy.md). A page line is not a bar.

Reload rule. When the crowd wants it again, the DJ pulls the record back and the MC starts over. Bars one and two carry the strongest line in the verse. If the opening needs three bars of setup to land, the verse dies on the first rewind.

Patterns, not chapters. One flow pattern holds for four or eight bars, then switches at a seam. A double-time section is announced: a held bar, a breath, a cue word the bar before. Surprise belongs in the punchline, never in the grid.

V2 moves the camera, the clock, or the room. Same rhyme map with swapped nouns is a corpse. In drill, V2 changes where we are standing and who just walked in.

No bridge. A drop-out bar, a rewind line, or a straight return to the hook does the job. Afroswing may take a four-bar melodic break; grime, drill, and hosting do not.

## Rhyme pressure

Grime: high. Multis, internals, slant chains, and the punchline on the end word. A particle hanging off the chime is a lazy bar.

Drill: end-hit locked. Short couplets, one clipped consonant family, a rhyme allowed to run four bars if the room changes. MC hosting: low, and the vowel matters more than the scheme because the crowd has to answer it. Afroswing: medium; hook clean, verse slant.

Forced rhyme here is American syntax bent so a London word can sit on the beat. Speech order first, then hang the chime off the true last stress.

## Pocket / mouth

Say it at tempo with a friend heckling. Weight goes to the front of the bar. Grime wants hard consonants and a short setup. Drill is staccato and breaks in threes and twos against the skip; few open vowels hang past the snare.

Rhyme on what the speaker says, not on the spelling. Glottal stops and th-fronting are the London mouth's. Do not spell the accent; choose the words and let the MC lean. Vowels dragged into American shape are the tell.

Hosting: open vowels for the sung turn, clipped shouts for the cue, air for the crowd. Afroswing: open vowels on the hook. A lilt, not a lullaby.

Reload test. Read bars one and two cold, twice. If the second pass has less punch, the setup is doing the work the punchline should.

## Flow parity

Verse syllables **off**, bar parity mandatory. Inside one pattern, syllables per bar stay in a band and the rhyme lands on the same beat. Switches land at seams (every four or eight bars, the reload point, the hook) and are named in CAST. Drill end-hits sit on the same beat in the same clipped consonant family.

Hook **strict**. MC hosting chants **strict** so the crowd can answer them: same words, same count, same shout vowel on the hit. Afroswing hooks match the held vowel on every return.

Count what the speaker says, and mark elisions on the page as [../flow.md](../flow.md) asks. London speech drops more than a dictionary does, so the mark is the contract.

Trap: a bar that doubles its syllables because a punchline needed room. Cut the setup, not the beat.

## POV & voice

First person default. Second person is the clash, the threat, or the host talking to the room. "We" only when the crew has a name in the brief.

Multicultural London English and patois are registers the writer must own. If the brief does not supply the register, write plain and flag it. Never coin slang: it ages in months and shifts by area.

Violence is a register, not a recruiting poster. Threat lives in rooms, routes, and consequences. No real victims, real rivals, or real incidents unless the brief supplies and owns them.

Money is this week's: the shop, the bus pass, the shift, the debt. Humour is a weapon.

## Hook

A chant or a stamp. Short, shoutable, and still alive when someone who heard it once yells it back.

It must survive the reload. Words do not change on return. A hook that rewrites itself every pass is a verse in disguise.

Hosting hook: the crowd call and the answer. Drill hook: a threat-mantra the room can chant without it rotting by cycle eight. Afroswing hook: sung, simple, patois only if a person sits behind it.

## Kill list

- Slang salad: bruv, innit, wasteman, peng sprinkled on American syntax
- "The ends" with no end named
- Fake postcode flex
- Americanised vowels on London words
- "They don't want to see me win" with no they
- Drill threat with no room, route, or logistics
- A bridge
- Explaining the metaphor in the next bar
- A hook that changes words every return
- Patois with no person behind it
- Crown and throne over a bedroom with no street named

## AI failure modes

American stream-of-consciousness drill with London nouns pasted on. Chicago and Brooklyn slang in a London mouth. Tourist-map London: landmarks instead of estates. Sixteen bars of one density with no punch in bar one. Switches that land mid-pattern for no reason, or none at all. A hosting verse written as a story. A grime hook sung like a pop chorus. Cartoon threat, cost-free flex, a moral tied off by the last bar. Metaphors that need a bar of gloss. Sanitised patois, or patois spelled by vibe.

If the verse survives a find-replace from London to Atlanta, it is not done.

## Hybrid note

Primary when the London mouth, reload-proof bars, or a hosting chant lead. `hip-hop.md` donates its kill list when bars are the product (throne, therapy-rap, motivational poster); `caribbean.md` donates its kill list when patois shows up, and its no-cosplay rule is law; `afro.md` donates its kill list for a melodic afroswing hook (buzzword stuffing, continent-as-aesthetic). Never put Chicago or Brooklyn drill slang and London slang in one mouth. Grime-garage hosting and sung afroswing are two jobs: pick one primary per song and do not average the two flows.
