# Caribbean

Locked pocket. Message, romance, or battle — pick. Register is earned from the artist, not from a vacation sticker pack. If you cannot source the slang, you do not get to invent it.

## Sub-lanes

- **Reggae (sung)** — spacey delivery, message-heavy or lover’s-rock intimacy. Clarity over crowded cleverness. Air is respect.
- **Lover’s rock** — close address, warmth, promise or ache. Still this pocket’s breath. Not a generic R&B sheet with island nouns.
- **Dancehall (deejay / toast)** — riddim-aware, punch and end-hits, catch phrases, half-time feel lines. Percussive mouth. Not sleepy melodic pop.

Singing vs deejay is a first-order choice. Wrong mouth ruins a correct theme.

## Form

Reggae: verse–chorus, room to reason. Do not pack a rap novel into a spacey sung brief. The hook is a mantra, slogan, or tender claim the crowd can return.

Dancehall: often a hard repetitive hook plus toasting verses. Breath on the riddim is form. Call-outs and catch phrases are structural, not clutter, when they are in-lane.

V2 should turn the argument or the flex, not reprint V1 with new destination names.

Do not write six unique dense sections. Repetition here is architecture.

## Rhyme pressure

Dancehall: high on punch and locked end-hits. Internal bounce welcome. Lazy ends feel like pop tourism.

Reggae: medium. Message must land. Scheme serves the proverb, not the other way around.

Do not invert into rhyme-Yoda. These lines get yelled or reasoned in a room.

If you are only writing English because you do not have the register, write clean, strong English. That is respectable. Fake Patois is not.

## Pocket / mouth

Leave air. Reggae that fills every sixteenth is a different genre wearing a postcard.

Dancehall needs percussive syllable timing and DJ-catchable fragments. Do not write long belted pop sentences for a deejay brief.

Open vowels on sung hooks; clipped consonants on toast. Do not hang sleepy melodic tails on a toasting job.

Speak it. If you need a glossary you invented, delete the glossary and the words.

## POV & voice

Reasoning voice, lover’s address, dancehall boast/battle/party, or social critique — as the brief’s ethics allow. First person default. Second person for love, challenge, or instruction. “We” when the community is real, not a tourism we.

Gun talk, battle, and certain party registers are ethics-gated. The brief’s hard lines win. Do not glam violence you were not asked to write, and do not lecture like a visiting NGO.

Babylon, Zion, and related vocabulary are thesis words, not confetti. If there is no argument, do not drop them as proof of authenticity.

Slang and register must be in-lane for *this* artist. When uncertain: plain correct speech. Never random spelling as “flavor.” Never a salad of keywords (*irie*, one love, island, sunshine) as a chorus.

## Hook

Mantra, slogan, dance instruction, or lover’s claim. Title as chant.

A crowd should be able to yell it without a sheet. If the hook is a paragraph, you are in the wrong pocket or the wrong sub-lane.

Do not stuff every geographic keyword into one refrain. One claim, held.

## Kill list

- Tourist “irie / one love” sticker pack
- Fake Patois by random spelling or vibe
- Babylon / Zion mentions with no thesis
- Keyword stuffing (island, sunshine, wave, ganja-as-personality)
- Sleepy pop sentences on a deejay brief
- Moral tourist lectures
- Register from three islands in one verse
- Costume Rasta without a reasoning argument
- Ignoring sing vs deejay
- Invented dialect from movies

## AI failure modes

Patois cosplay. Reggae as stoner postcard. Dancehall without riddim breath. NGO lecture. English pop structure with a coconut. Every line a slogan, none a thought. Slang salad. No pocket. A chorus that would also work for a cruise ad.

If a Jamaican artist would wince at the spelling, you already failed ethics and craft.
