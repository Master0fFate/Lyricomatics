# Song economy

Open before every draft or full rewrite. This is the antidote to the model habit of writing a small novel because nobody stopped it.

Economy is not “make everything short.” It is **give each section one job, spend only the lines needed to do it, and preserve space for music**.

## Count the right thing

A lyric line is one **sung phrase**: a complete thought, or an intentional suspension that the next phrase resolves. It is not every comma. It is not whatever fits the chat window.

Without a supplied melody:

- Treat each displayed line as one provisional sung phrase.
- Use one or two breath groups per line.
- Do not claim an exact bar count, stress fit, or singability. Those need music.
- Prefer balanced phrase lengths in repeated sections. Verse lines may vary more.

With a supplied melody, demo, scansion, or bar map: that source wins. Match its phrase slots, stresses, pickups, holds, and rests.

## Start lean

Use these as **first-draft budgets**, not laws. Genre, era, tempo, melody, and form can override them. Exceed a range only when every extra line supplies a new beat that the section needs.

| Lane / form | Verse | Pre | Chorus / refrain | Bridge / turn |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Current radio pop / pop-rock | 4–6 lines | 2–4 | 2–6 | 2–4 |
| R&B / soul | 4–6 | 0–4 | 2–6 | 2–4 |
| Rock / metal | 4–8 | 0–4 | 2–6 | 2–6 |
| Country radio | 4–8 | 0–4 | 4–6 | 0–4 |
| Folk / narrative country | 4–8 per stanza | rare | 1–4 refrain lines | often a final verse instead |
| Rap | 8, 12, or 16 bars when requested; lines follow flow | rare | 2–8 bars | only if the form turns |
| Punk / hardcore | 2–6 | rare | 1–4 | usually a break, not an essay |
| House / EDM / amapiano mantra | 0–4 unique lines | 0–2 | 1–4 unique lines | usually none |
| Afrobeats / reggae / dancehall | 2–6 | 0–2 | 1–4 | usually none |
| Blues AAB | 3 lyric statements across a 12-bar stanza | — | the A line is the hook | — |
| Jazz / standard AABA | melody controls; often 8-bar sections | — | title/refrain inside A | B is the turn |
| Theatre / through-composed | scene and melody control | form-specific | need statement or motif | plot/character turn |
| Corrido / ballad / long-form story | 6–12 when facts require it | rare | 1–4 refrain lines | often a final narrative turn |

A line may occupy more than one bar. A bar may contain more than one short phrase. Do not pretend page lines and bars are identical.

## Whole-song budget

Count **unique** lyric lines, not repeated choruses.

- Mainstream verse/chorus song: start near 14–24 unique lines.
- Sparse groove, chant, punk, dance, or vamp: often 4–16.
- Story, theatre, conscious rap, corrido, folk ballad: may need 20–40 or more.

These are diagnostic ranges. A long song must earn duration through event, turn, character, or verbal performance. A short song must still feel complete in its chosen form.

## The section card

Before ink, make a silent card:

```text
Form:
Era target:
V1 job / budget:
Pre job / budget:
Hook job / budget:
V2 move / budget:
Turn, if any / budget:
Title landing:
```

No section gets two jobs because the writer was indecisive.

## Compression pass

Draft the section. Then compress it in this order:

1. Cut the line that explains the line before it.
2. Cut the second image that proves the same fact.
3. Cut throat-clearing before the first charged verb or noun.
4. Merge setup with action when the mouth stays natural.
5. Keep the best line, not the line required to rescue a weak rhyme.
6. Move context the chorus does not need into a verse.
7. If a verse exceeds its budget, name the new beat in every extra line. No named beat means no line.

Target: remove roughly 15–25% from an overfull first draft. Do not cut a narrative turn, necessary setup, or character-specific grain merely to satisfy a number.

## Repetition with consequence

Chart history supports repetition, but repetition is not cloning filler.

- Repeat the chorus or refrain substantially unchanged so the listener can learn it.
- Let the new verse change what the same chorus means.
- Vary the final chorus only when one changed word or line pays off the song.
- Repeat a title because it gains force, creates rhythm, or invites participation.
- Do not repeat because the section ran out of thought.

The useful tension is **familiar frame + distinctive content**. A hook should be fluent enough to retain and particular enough to belong only to this song.

## Verse tests

### Verse 1

By its last line, V1 should establish only what the hook needs:

- who or where we are
- the pressure or event
- the ramp into the hook

It does not need the entire backstory.

### Verse 2

V2 changes at least one axis: time, place, evidence, power, knowledge, or consequence. It is often equal to or shorter than V1.

Do not add a third example of the same wound. Two strong pieces of evidence beat five synonyms.

### Long verse permission

A verse may run long when the form is built for verbal or narrative accumulation: rap, corrido, ballad, theatre, comic list, talking blues, or through-composed work. Then require a turn, escalation, reveal, or punch at least every 2–4 lines or 4 bars. Length without internal motion is only acreage.

## Hook economy

A hook needs less information than a verse and more identity.

Check:

- Can a listener repeat its core after one pass?
- Is the title or central phrase in a strong position?
- Does the hook make one primary move: plea, claim, command, question, verdict, celebration, accusation, prayer?
- Is there one line too many after the payoff?
- Does the lyric leave room for the title to be held, repeated, answered, or interrupted?

If the chorus needs a paragraph to be understood, the verse failed to set it up or the chorus is doing the verse’s job.

## Era changes the budget

- **Standards / Tin Pan Alley:** compact AABA or verse-refrain forms; title often anchors the A section; craft can be verbally dense because the melody maps it tightly.
- **Blues, folk, early country:** strophic or AAB recurrence; the repeated frame carries new verses. Do not bolt on a modern pre-chorus.
- **1950s–1970s rock, soul, pop:** verse/chorus, refrain, AABA, call-and-response, and extended story forms coexist. Do not flatten history into one radio template.
- **1980s–2000s mainstream:** larger sectional contrast, pre-choruses, bridges, and repeated final choruses are common, but not compulsory.
- **Streaming-era mainstream:** faster identity, fewer unique sections, and leaner verses are common. This is a market tendency, not a commandment.

When the user names an era, honor its form and diction. “Timeless” does not mean modern pop with old furniture.

## Stop rule

A section is finished when it has done its job and created desire for the next section.

Do not add a line because the page looks small. Music is allowed to exist. Humanity survives another silence.
