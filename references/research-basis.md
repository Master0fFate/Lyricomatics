# Research basis and limits

Maintainer reference. Do not load this file during normal drafting. It explains which Lyricomatics rules are evidence-backed, which are craft consensus, and which are adaptable defaults.

## What chart evidence can prove

Billboard rank measures market performance under the chart rules of its era. It does **not** isolate lyric quality. Melody, artist fame, label spend, distribution, format, radio, streaming, social context, and luck all matter.

Therefore:

- Use chart research to find recurring tendencies, not a recipe for guaranteed hits.
- Do not treat correlation as causation.
- Do not apply modern chart tendencies to blues, standards, folk ballads, theatre, or other forms that have different jobs.
- Do not call a preference “universal” when the data covers only English-language Western popular music.
- Do not optimize lexical simplicity into blandness. Fluency and differentiation must coexist.

## Findings that inform the skill

### Repetition can improve fluency and market reach

Nunes, Ordanini, and Valsesia used controlled studies plus Billboard Hot 100 data from 1958–2012. More repetitive lyrics were processed more fluently; in their chart analysis, repetition was associated with a better chance of reaching number one, faster ascent, and a higher chance of entering the Top 40.

**Skill implication:** repeat strong hooks and stable refrains. Do not “improve” every chorus into new wording. Repetition must carry a worthy line; it cannot rescue a generic one.

Source: J. C. Nunes, A. Ordanini, and F. Valsesia, “The power of repetition: repetitive lyrics in a song increase processing fluency and drive market success,” *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 25 (2015), 187–199. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.12.004

### Popular lyrics have trended toward greater compressibility

Varnum et al. analyzed 14,661 songs that entered the Billboard Hot 100 from 1958–2016. Average lyrical compressibility increased over time, and simpler/more compressible entries achieved higher chart positions in their model, especially when more novel songs competed for attention.

**Skill implication:** use semantic compression and recognizable recurrence in mainstream work. Do not infer that all strong lyrics must be short, literal, or low-vocabulary.

Source: M. E. W. Varnum et al., “Why are song lyrics becoming simpler? A time series analysis of lyrical complexity in six decades of American popular music,” *PLOS ONE* 16 (2021), e0244576. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0244576

### Genre and era materially change the result

Parada-Cabaleiro et al. analyzed 353,320 English lyrics across pop, rock, rap, R&B, and country. They found long-term movement toward more repeated lines, a larger chorus share, more personal language, and lower lexical complexity, with important differences by genre. Their dataset and automatic descriptors also have limits: lyric transcription, section labels, and platform populations are imperfect.

**Skill implication:** route by genre and era. Never turn a population trend into a hard per-song line count.

Source: E. Parada-Cabaleiro et al., “Song lyrics have become simpler and more repetitive over the last five decades,” *Scientific Reports* 14, 5531 (2024). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-55742-x

### Difference inside a recognizable category matters

Berger and Packard used natural-language processing on thousands of songs. Lyrics with topics that were more differentiated from their genre were more popular; the effect was weaker in dance and pop and applied to lyrical topic rather than stylistic surface.

**Skill implication:** keep the genre’s mouth and form, but give the song a premise, object, behavior, title, or perspective that nearby songs do not have. “Sound like the lane” and “say the lane’s default thing” are not the same instruction.

Source: J. Berger and G. Packard, “Are atypical things more popular?” *Psychological Science* 29 (2018), 1178–1184. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618759465

### Prosody is alignment, not meter policing

Pat Pattison’s songwriting pedagogy defines prosody broadly: lyric, rhythm, rhyme, melody, harmony, and section motion should support the central emotional purpose. His language work emphasizes preserving natural speech stress and matching lyric phrases to melodic phrases.

**Skill implication:** meaning words take strong positions; natural word stress matters; stable and unstable rhyme, rhythm, and section shape can support stable or unstable emotion. Without melody, any singability judgment stays provisional.

Sources:

- Pat Pattison, “Prosody in Music and Songwriting,” Berklee Online, 2022. https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/prosody-in-music-and-songwriting/
- Pat Pattison, “Language and Songwriting.” https://www.patpattison.com/language-and-songwriting
- Pat Pattison, *Writing Better Lyrics*, 2nd ed. (Writer’s Digest Books, 2009).

### Repetition creates expectation, participation, and changing meaning

Work in music cognition by Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis and David Huron shows that repetition is central to musical expectation and perceived participation. Repetition can increase fluency, but habituation requires contrast or recontextualization.

**Skill implication:** repeat the hook; change its meaning through the verse, delivery, addressee, or one late variation. Contrast can come from section density, syntax, POV, rhyme stability, or information—not only from new words.

Sources:

- Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis, *On Repeat: How Music Plays the Mind* (Oxford University Press, 2014). https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199990825.001.0001
- David Huron, “A Psychological Approach to Musical Form: The Habituation-Fluency Theory of Repetition,” *Current Musicology* 96 (2013), 7–35. https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/D8KP81FG

### Concrete language is a tool, not a religion

Choi and Downie found a historical increase in lyric concreteness in their corpus. Concrete detail can improve scene, recall, and distinctiveness, but computational concreteness does not measure emotional truth, metaphor quality, or singability.

**Skill implication:** use concrete action and sensory detail where a section needs proof. Let hooks use emotionally direct or abstract language when the verses have earned it. Never force a brand, timestamp, or kitchen prop into every song.

Source: K. Choi and J. S. Downie, “A Trend Analysis on Concreteness of Popular Song Lyrics,” DLfM 2019. https://doi.org/10.1145/3358664.3358673

## Historical form sources

These sources support the form router rather than exact hit formulas:

- Alec Wilder, *American Popular Song: The Great Innovators, 1900–1950* (Oxford University Press). Standards frequently use compact sectional forms such as AABA and verse-refrain, with melody and title placement tightly linked.
- John Covach, “Form in Rock Music: A Primer,” in *Engaging Music* (Oxford University Press, 2005). Rock-era form includes AABA, verse-chorus, simple verse, contrasting verse-chorus, bridges, and hybrids.
- Jay Summach, “Form in Top-20 Rock Music, 1955–89” (PhD diss., Yale University, 2012). Mainstream forms and section functions changed across decades; the prechorus and sectional cycle have histories rather than timeless rules.
- Nathaniel Condit-Schultz, “MCFlow: A Digital Corpus of Rap Transcriptions,” *Empirical Musicology Review* 11 (2016), 124–147. Rap flow and rhyme require timing-aware analysis beyond page-end rhyme. https://doi.org/10.18061/emr.v11i2.4961

## Craft consensus, not scientific law

The following are professional defaults because they reliably diagnose drafts. They are not empirically proven guarantees:

- A section should have a clear job.
- V2 should change time, place, evidence, knowledge, power, or consequence.
- The bridge should turn the song or stay absent.
- A title should occupy a memorable structural position.
- End words should carry meaning.
- One dominant image world usually creates coherence, but a deliberate counter-image can create the turn.
- The strongest emotional line often describes behavior under pressure instead of naming the emotion.
- Lean first drafts are easier to deepen than bloated drafts are to sing.

Treat these as knives, not commandments.

## Product doctrine

Lyricomatics optimizes for five things at once:

1. **Fluency** — the mouth and memory can hold it.
2. **Identity** — the premise and language belong to this song.
3. **Motion** — sections change pressure, knowledge, or meaning.
4. **Fit** — genre, era, character, and melody agree.
5. **Restraint** — the lyric leaves room for voice, music, and the listener.

A chart-shaped lyric without identity is product paste. A beautiful poem without musical fit is still not a song. The skill must refuse both forms of vanity.
